Two profiles. One question. Repeat.

The measurement at the centre of the product. It's easier than swiping, more accurate than ranking, and it produces something neither can: a complete preference ordering over everyone in the pool, plus a running measure of which attributes actually drive choice.

Why not just ask people to rank everyone?

The instinct is that a full ranking of all 250 candidates would be the most accurate possible measurement. It isn't — and the reason is cognitive rather than technical.

DOESN'T SCALE

Ranking 250 people

A ranked list requires holding the whole set in mind at once. To place person 147 correctly you'd need to recall where 1 through 146 sat and slot them precisely between two of them.

People can't do this past roughly seven to ten items. Past that, what you get isn't careful judgment — it's noise wearing the costume of precision.

A complete list produced this way is less accurate than a short one done properly.

LOSES INFORMATION

Swiping through 250 people

A swipe is binary. Yes or no tells you nothing about whether someone prefers this yes to that yes.

It cannot produce an ordering — which is precisely why no existing swipe app could run deferred acceptance even if it wanted to.

Fast, effortless, and structurally unable to answer the question.

Pairwise comparison does both jobs

Each judgment is independent and needs nothing except the two people on screen. No memory burden at all. Someone can make 500 of these across a month without fatigue, where ranking 250 people is a chore nobody finishes.

And it produces the complete ranking anyway. The Bradley-Terry model takes pairwise choices and estimates a preference score for everyone in the pool — including pairs never directly compared. Chose Marcus over Tom, and Tom over Dev? The model infers Marcus over Dev without asking.

Same mathematics as chess Elo ratings. Nobody plays every opponent, and everyone still ends up with a rating that predicts outcomes well.

Roughly 20 to 30 comparisons per person yields a reliable top ten, using adaptive selection — showing comparisons where the model is currently uncertain rather than obvious ones. More is better with sharply diminishing returns; by comparison 100 you're mostly confirming what the model already knows. And it spreads across weeks perfectly well, since Bradley-Terry combines comparisons regardless of when they were made.

Why twenty and not two hundred

Twenty comparisons gets you roughly 80% of the way to a ranking matching what someone would produce by hand. Doubling to forty adds about six points. Going all the way to two hundred adds two more.

Illustrative — shows the shape of diminishing returns in adaptive pairwise sampling, not measured results from a live pool.

The bonus nobody else can collect

Every comparison is also a data point about which attributes drove the choice. You know A's characteristics, you know B's, and you know who won.

That's conjoint analysis — the method market researchers use to decompose preferences into the weight placed on each attribute. Run at scale it measures what people actually weight, versus what they said they wanted at baseline.

Which is the height-and-age override finding, converted from an anecdote into an instrument. When The League overrode men's stated age preferences and women's stated height preferences by a small margin, match rates rose on both sides. This design measures that gap systematically, with outcomes attached.

    Experiment one: does matching work at all?

    Everyone's preference data is collected identically. What happens to it is randomised, and nobody is told which arm they're in — the interface is the same in all four, only the back end differs.

    Arm 1

    Deferred acceptance

    Gale-Shapley on the estimated rankings. Which side proposes is itself randomised, since the proposing side does better.

    Arm 2

    Attribute similarity

    Matched on shared baseline characteristics. What the industry actually sells today.

    Arm 3

    Random within filters

    Age, location, orientation. Nothing else. The control that matters most.

    Arm 4

    Free choice

    Browse and pick, as on any existing app. The status quo and the fairest commercial comparison.

    Attrition kills this study, not sample size. At five per cent annual dropout you keep six in ten people after a decade; at fifteen per cent you keep two.
